What counts as compensated

A gas is partially compensated when the second value has moved in the direction that drags the pH back toward normal — the lungs blowing off CO₂ for a metabolic problem, or the kidneys adjusting bicarbonate for a respiratory one. If that value still sits inside its own reference range, nothing has compensated yet.

Every gas here is partially compensated at most, never fully. A fully compensated gas has a normal pH, and a normal pH cannot tell you which disorder came first.

Reading a gas

1. Look at the pH

Below 7.35 is acidaemia, above 7.45 is alkalaemia. The pH tells you which side the primary problem sits on, because compensation never overshoots — it only ever drags the pH back toward normal.

2. Find the culprit

In acidaemia: a high PaCO₂ means respiratory acidosis, a low HCO₃⁻ means metabolic acidosis. In alkalaemia: a low PaCO₂ means respiratory alkalosis, a high HCO₃⁻ means metabolic alkalosis.

3. Check the other value

If it has moved in the direction that pushes the pH back toward normal, that is compensation — answer partially compensated. If it sits inside its own reference range, answer uncompensated.

Lungs compensate for metabolic problems within minutes. Kidneys compensate for respiratory problems over days.

4. PaO₂

Always report it in practice, but here it never changes the acid–base answer.

Ranges used in this game

pH 7.35–7.45 · PaO₂ 10–13 kPa · PaCO₂ 4.7–6.0 kPa · HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mmol/L

One thing this game will never ask

A fully compensated gas cannot be interpreted from numbers alone: a normal pH with a high PaCO₂ and a high HCO₃⁻ could be a compensated respiratory acidosis or a compensated metabolic alkalosis, and only the history separates them. Every gas here has an abnormal pH for that reason.
